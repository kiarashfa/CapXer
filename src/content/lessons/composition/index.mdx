## What composition is

Tate says the term composition usually refers to the arrangement of elements within a work of art. An artist arranges the different elements so that they sit in a relationship that satisfies the artist and, it is hoped, the viewer [@tate-composition, para. 1]. Kodak's glossary gives a picture-making version: the distribution, balance and relationship of masses, light and shade, line and colour within a picture area [@kodak-essential-reference-guide, p. 193].

Nikon's primer says composition covers what the photographer chooses to include, what they leave out and how the elements appear in the frame [@nikon-essential-composition-techniques, sec. The role of composition in photography]. Tate was describing painting, where the traditional idea is adjusting the relationships of the elements within the border of the canvas [@tate-composition, para. 3].

The famous "rules" are usually taught as guidelines. Nikon says they are not hard and fast, but guidelines that make you familiar enough with how a photo works to bend or break them creatively [@nikon-essential-composition-techniques, sec. Use this guide as a starting point]. A film-making textbook agrees that there are times to break its composition rules, and that understanding them helps you break them effectively [@reid-sanders-2021-documentary-making-ch10, p. 104]. Nikon says grid lines are a guide to help you frame your image and do not show up in the final picture [@nikon-5-easy-composition-guidelines, sec. The Rule of Thirds], and a college video handbook says the same of cameras that overlay grid lines without recording them [@libretexts-ccsf-video-composition, sec. Rule of Thirds].

## The rule of thirds

John Thomas Smith used the name "Rule of thirds" in 1797, in his book *Remarks on Rural Scenery* [@smith-remarks-rural-scenery-1797, sec. Advertisement; @smith-remarks-rural-scenery-1797, p. 16]. He had observed that in pictures by the best masters, particularly Rembrandt, the principal light is placed near the middle of the scene with above two-thirds of the picture in shadow [@smith-remarks-rural-scenery-1797, p. 15]. He called that proportion a "Rule of thirds", with an apology for the name, and proposed a similar scheme of proportion for connecting or breaking the lines of a picture [@smith-remarks-rural-scenery-1797, p. 16].

Smith's rule is not a nine-box grid. For a landscape he suggested putting the sky at about two-thirds, or else at about one-third, so that the material objects occupy the other two. He found that "a much better and more harmonizing proportion" than the precise formal half [@smith-remarks-rural-scenery-1797, p. 16]. He offered the idea tentatively and said he would keep to it until he was better informed [@smith-remarks-rural-scenery-1797, p. 17].

By 1972 a textbook for television cameramen taught a grid version. It said the geometric centre is not the best place for the centre of interest. Divide the picture into thirds both horizontally and vertically, it said, and the points of intersection are the most suitable places for points of interest, though the author admitted it is difficult to explain why [@jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 81]. Nikon teaches it today as an imagined tic-tac-toe grid over the scene, whose intersections are suggested positions for the subject [@nikon-5-easy-composition-guidelines, sec. The Rule of Thirds].

The grid has other uses. A Nikon photographer describes it as nine equal rectangles and advises placing the subject off-centre, along the lines or where they meet [@nikon-7-composition-grids, sec. The rule of thirds: start with the fundamentals]. The same lines help keep horizons level and vertical elements straight [@nikon-5-easy-composition-guidelines, sec. The Rule of Thirds]. Nikon also advises putting the horizon above or below the middle of the frame rather than dead centre [@nikon-5-easy-composition-guidelines, sec. Where to place the horizon line]. For interviews, a video handbook suggests placing the eyes on a top left or right intersection, which it says makes a more interesting shot than simply placing them in the centre [@libretexts-ccsf-video-composition, sec. Rule of Thirds].

## The golden ratio

The golden ratio has an exact definition. Euclid wrote that a straight line is cut in extreme and mean ratio when the whole line is to the greater segment as the greater is to the less [@euclid-elements-joyce, sec. Definition 3]. Markowsky puts it the same way, and for a segment of length 1 the larger piece is about 0.618, so the ratio is (1 + the square root of 5) / 2, about 1.618 [@markowsky-1992-misconceptions, p. 2]. Guide lines placed by this ratio fall at 61.8% of the way across and, from the other side, at 38.2%. Those lines sit only 4.9 points of the side away from the thirds lines at 33.3% and 66.7%.

</Figure>

The idea has many names. Markowsky lists golden section, golden number, golden mean and divine proportion [@markowsky-1992-misconceptions, p. 2]. The name "golden ratio" itself is a nineteenth-century creation and not an ancient name [@markowsky-2005-livio-review, p. 344]. The classical "golden mean" meant something else, the avoidance of excess in either direction [@markowsky-1992-misconceptions, p. 5].

Many popular accounts say the Parthenon and Leonardo da Vinci used this ratio [@markowsky-1992-misconceptions, p. 8; @markowsky-1992-misconceptions, p. 10], and Markowsky disputes both. He treated ratios between 1.58 and 1.66 as close enough to be worth considering, though not as proof that the ratio is present [@markowsky-1992-misconceptions, p. 6], and found that the Parthenon dimensions he cites give ratios of about 2.25 [@markowsky-1992-misconceptions, p. 9]. Of Leonardo, he writes that biographies give no indication that he used the golden ratio in paintings or drawings not intended for Pacioli's book [@markowsky-1992-misconceptions, p. 11]. In 2005 he added that he had found no credible evidence that Greek artists and architects ever used it for any project [@markowsky-2005-livio-review, p. 345]. His 1992 paper concluded that the claims made for its aesthetic importance seem to be without foundation [@markowsky-1992-misconceptions, p. 15]. Tests of whether people prefer such rectangles, quoted in the same paper, gave results that were on the whole negative [@markowsky-1992-misconceptions, p. 13].

Some photography teaching uses it anyway. A Nikon photographer describes the golden spiral as a visualisation of the golden ratio that guides the eye through the frame [@nikon-7-composition-grids, sec. The golden spiral: for effortless flow], and offers a "phi grid" as an alternative to thirds [@nikon-7-composition-grids, sec. The phi grid: a smarter rule of thirds]. The older television textbook had its own view. In 1972 its author wrote that the Greek "Golden Mean" put centres of interest five-eighths of the way in from the edges of the frame, and that five-eighths is almost two thirds, so thirds are close enough for practical purposes [@jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 81; @jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 82]. Five-eighths is 62.5%, which is close to the 61.8% golden line.

## Centre and symmetry

One older broadcast textbook is wary of the middle. In 1972 it said that framing artists and objects symmetrically rarely gives a satisfactory picture, because the effect is too formal [@jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 80]. It called a two-shot with both people shoulder to shoulder facing the camera the most unattractive kind, since both command equal attention [@jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 105].

Nikon's current teaching is less strict. It notes that advice to new photographers is usually to avoid the middle of the frame [@nikon-center-composition-portrait, para. 1], but that centre composition can be striking when used thoughtfully [@nikon-center-composition-portrait, para. 2]. Its primer says centring creates symmetry and subconsciously tells the viewer what the subject is [@nikon-essential-composition-techniques, sec. Centring the subject and the rule of thirds]. A food photographer adds that placing the subject smack in the centre can give it importance and a bold, impactful look [@nikon-7-composition-grids, sec. Use symmetry to convey importance].

A centred shot asks more of the subject and the rest of the frame. Nikon advises making sure the subject is striking enough to command the frame [@nikon-center-composition-portrait, sec. 1. Make sure that your subject is striking enough] and keeping the rest of the frame as clean as possible [@nikon-center-composition-portrait, sec. 2. Keep the rest of your frame as clean as possible], and a centred horizon can suit a reflection [@nikon-5-easy-composition-guidelines, sec. Where to place the horizon line].

## Headroom and lead room

Headroom is the amount of space between the top of the artist's head and the top of the frame [@jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 97]. A film-making textbook says a head touching the top of the frame feels confined [@reid-sanders-2021-documentary-making-ch10, p. 104], while giving more than a third of the frame to headroom can leave a subject feeling lost amid their surroundings [@reid-sanders-2021-documentary-making-ch10, p. 105]. A video handbook makes the same point: too much headroom makes the subject look less important [@libretexts-ccsf-video-composition, sec. Headroom].

For placing the eyes, the 1972 textbook gave a rule of thumb. Eyes should never be placed exactly half-way up the frame [@jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 97], which normally leads to excessive headroom [@jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 98]. They should sit between the centre line and a point never higher than two-thirds of the distance up [@jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 98]. For a full-length shot it suggested twice as much headroom as footroom [@jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 102]. It also warned that home viewers did not see the whole picture the cameraman saw, with the greatest cut-off at the top, so headroom that looked right in the viewfinder could be too little on the screen [@jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 97]. That was analogue television in the 1970s.

Looking room is the space the cameraman leaves between a subject's eyes and the edge of the frame on the side the subject looks towards [@jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 108]. The same book says a profile needs more of it than a semi-profile [@jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 108]. An open textbook says audiences unconsciously demand it [@reid-sanders-2021-documentary-making-ch10, p. 107]. Breaking the rule can be deliberate: leaving no looking room can convey isolation or loneliness [@jones-technique-television-cameraman-1972, p. 109].

When the subject moves, the term is lead room. A video handbook separates nose room, the area in front of the subject [@libretexts-ccsf-video-composition, sec. Nose Room], from lead room, which refers to a subject in motion, and says to give more room in front of the motion than behind it [@libretexts-ccsf-video-composition, sec. Lead Room]. Nikon gives the same advice for photographs: leave more space on the side of the frame where the action is headed [@nikon-5-easy-composition-guidelines, sec. Lean Into the Frame].

## The frame shape and guides

The guides depend on the frame you choose. Kodak's 2007 guide lists 1.33:1 as the standard TV and video ratio, written 4:3 in the television industry [@kodak-essential-reference-guide, p. 37]. ARRI calls 2.39:1 the traditional aspect ratio of CinemaScope [@arri-alexa-lf-anamorphic-2018, p. 9]. Measured against the frame height, the vertical thirds lines sit at 0.797 and 1.593 on 2.39:1 but at 0.444 and 0.889 on 4:3 (2.39 / 3 and 1.333 / 3), so on the wide frame they are much further apart. Drawn at the same width instead, the 2.39:1 frame is only 0.558 times as tall as the 4:3 frame (1.333 / 2.39), so its horizontal lines are closer together.

A crop moves the edges of the frame. Kodak says theatre owners made 1.85:1 by cutting off the top and bottom of the 1.37:1 image [@kodak-essential-reference-guide, p. 37]. A Nikon photographer notes that grids can be used while shooting or as a crop tool in post-production [@nikon-7-composition-grids, sec. How to use these grids].

Broadcast adds safe areas, which keep essential action away from the edge: in 2017 the EBU asked 16:9 programme makers, where appropriate, to keep it inside an Action Safe Area [@ebu-r95-2017, p. 3]. The margins are covered in [Aspect ratios](../aspect-ratios/).

## Composing a shot

<OurView>

Use the guides to look, not to decide. Switch on the thirds lines, place the subject, then ask whether the picture needs the subject there. A line in the viewfinder cannot know what your shot is about. If thirds and golden lines both seem to fit, pick whichever makes the shot feel balanced; the difference between them is small.

Decide the frame shape first, because every guide depends on it. Then check three things before you roll. Is there enough room above the head, and in front of a face that looks or a person who walks? Is the horizon level and clear of the middle? Is there a reason for anything you have centred? If the answer is yes, centre it on purpose and keep the rest of the frame clean. Rules are a starting point, and once you know why they work you can break them.

</OurView>
